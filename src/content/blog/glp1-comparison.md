## Introduction

**Medical disclaimer:** This article is for research and educational purposes only. It does not constitute medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment recommendation. GLP-1 receptor agonists are potent prescription medicines with significant physiological effects and documented risks. Self-administration without medical supervision is dangerous and potentially life-threatening. Always consult a qualified South African healthcare professional before considering any peptide-based therapy. The regulatory status of these compounds varies; using unregistered medicines carries legal, financial, and health risks.

The landscape of metabolic pharmacotherapy has shifted dramatically over the past five years. What began with liraglutide — a daily injectable delivering modest weight loss — has evolved into a class of once-weekly peptides capable of producing weight reductions previously associated only with bariatric surgery. For South African researchers and clinicians tracking this space, three molecules dominate the conversation: **semaglutide** (Novo Nordisk), **tirzepatide** (Eli Lilly), and **retatrutide** (Eli Lilly).

This article provides a direct, evidence-based comparison of these three agents. We will examine their mechanisms, clinical efficacy, side effect profiles, dosing protocols, cost implications, and — critically — their availability and regulatory status within South Africa. We will not oversell. These are not miracle compounds. They are powerful tools with meaningful limitations, significant individual variation in response, and real safety concerns that demand respect.

## Mechanism of Action: One Receptor vs Two vs Three

The fundamental difference between these three peptides lies in which hormone receptors they activate. This is not a minor technical distinction — it drives the differences in efficacy, side effects, and metabolic outcomes we see in clinical trials.

### Semaglutide: GLP-1 Receptor Agonist Only

Semaglutide is a long-acting analog of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), a gut hormone released after meals. It binds selectively to the GLP-1 receptor (GLP-1R) and exerts its effects through three primary mechanisms [1]:

1. **Appetite suppression** — Acts on GLP-1 receptors in the hypothalamus and brainstem to reduce hunger signaling and increase satiety
2. **Gastric emptying delay** — Slows food transit from stomach to small intestine, prolonging fullness
3. **Glucose-dependent insulin secretion** — Stimulates insulin release only when blood glucose is elevated, reducing hypoglycemia risk

Semaglutide does **not** activate GIP receptors or glucagon receptors. Its effects are mediated purely through GLP-1R signaling. This single-receptor approach is well-characterized after two decades of GLP-1 research, but it also represents a ceiling — one that dual and triple agonists appear to break through.

### Tirzepatide: Dual GIP/GLP-1 Receptor Agonist

Tirzepatide represents the next evolutionary step. It is a dual agonist, activating both the glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor and the GLP-1 receptor [2].

The addition of GIP receptor agonism matters for several reasons:

- **Enhanced insulin secretion** — GIP and GLP-1 act synergistically on pancreatic beta cells, producing greater glucose-stimulated insulin release than either alone
- **Possible adipose tissue effects** — GIP receptor activation on adipocytes may improve lipid storage dynamics and insulin sensitivity in fat tissue
- **Potential anti-nausea effect** — Some evidence suggests GIP signaling may counteract GLP-1–mediated nausea, allowing higher tolerated effective doses
- **Reduced tachyphylaxis** — Dual-receptor signaling may produce less receptor desensitization over time, sustaining efficacy longer

The exact contribution of GIP agonism to tirzepatide's superior weight loss remains debated. What is not debated is the clinical result: tirzepatide consistently outperforms semaglutide in head-to-head and cross-trial comparisons.

### Retatrutide: Triple GIP/GLP-1/Glucagon Receptor Agonist

Retatrutide adds a third receptor target: the glucagon receptor (GCGR). This makes it the first triple hormone receptor agonist to reach late-stage clinical development [3].

The glucagon component introduces a metabolic dimension absent in the other two agents:

- **Increased energy expenditure** — Glucagon receptor activation in the liver increases hepatic glucose output and may raise resting metabolic rate through thermogenesis
- **Enhanced lipolysis** — Glucagon promotes fat oxidation, potentially improving body composition beyond what weight loss alone would achieve
- **Hepatic fat reduction** — Early data suggests retatrutide produces substantial reductions in liver fat content, relevant for MASH/NAFLD

However, glucagon also raises blood glucose. The triple agonist design attempts to balance this by coupling glucagon receptor activation with the glucose-lowering effects of GIP and GLP-1. In practice, retatrutide lowers fasting glucose and HbA1c despite the glucagon component — but the long-term implications of chronic low-grade glucagon receptor activation are not yet fully characterized.

| Feature | Semaglutide | Tirzepatide | Retatrutide |
|---------|-------------|-------------|-------------|
| **Receptor targets** | GLP-1R only | GIPR + GLP-1R | GIPR + GLP-1R + GCGR |
| **Primary metabolic effect** | Appetite suppression + gastric delay | Appetite + enhanced insulin secretion | Appetite + insulin + energy expenditure |
| **Energy expenditure effect** | Minimal direct effect | Minimal direct effect | Likely increased via glucagon |
| **Hepatic fat reduction** | Moderate | Moderate | Substantial (early data) |
| **Development stage** | Approved, widely available | Approved, limited SA availability | Phase 3, investigational |

## Clinical Trial Data: What the Numbers Actually Show

Cross-trial comparisons are inherently imperfect. Different populations, different durations, different background treatments, and different analytical methods all introduce noise. That said, the magnitude of difference between these agents is large enough that meaningful ranking is possible.

### Weight Loss Efficacy

| Trial | Agent | Dose | Mean Weight Loss | Duration | N | Population |
|-------|-------|------|------------------|----------|---|------------|
| STEP 1 [4] | Semaglutide | 2.4 mg | **14.9%** | 68 weeks | 1,961 | Obesity, no diabetes |
| STEP 2 [5] | Semaglutide | 2.4 mg | **9.6%** | 68 weeks | 1,210 | Obesity + T2D |
| STEP 5 [6] | Semaglutide | 2.4 mg | **15.2%** | 104 weeks | 304 | Obesity, no diabetes |
| SURMOUNT-1 [7] | Tirzepatide | 15 mg | **20.9%** | 72 weeks | 2,539 | Obesity, no diabetes |
| SURMOUNT-2 [8] | Tirzepatide | 15 mg | **14.7%** | 72 weeks | 938 | Obesity + T2D |
| SURMOUNT-5 [9] | Tirzepatide | 15 mg | **20.2%** | 72 weeks | 751 | Head-to-head vs semaglutide |
| SURMOUNT-5 [9] | Semaglutide | 2.4 mg | **13.7%** | 72 weeks | 751 | Head-to-head vs tirzepatide |
| Phase 2 [3] | Retatrutide | 12 mg | **24.2%** | 48 weeks | 338 | Obesity, no diabetes |
| TRIUMPH-1 [10] | Retatrutide | 12 mg | **28.3%** | 80 weeks | ~3,000 | Obesity, no diabetes (Phase 3) |
| TRIUMPH-1 [10] | Retatrutide | 12 mg | **30.3%** | 104 weeks | 532 (extension) | BMI ≥35 subgroup |

**Key observations:**

1. **Semaglutide delivers ~15% weight loss** in non-diabetic populations over 68–104 weeks. This is substantial and clinically meaningful, but it is the lowest of the three.

2. **Tirzepatide delivers ~20% weight loss** at maximum dose — roughly 5–7 percentage points more than semaglutide. The SURMOUNT-5 head-to-head trial confirmed this directly: tirzepatide produced 47% greater total weight loss than semaglutide when both were used at their maximum approved doses [9].

3. **Retatrutide is in a different category.** Even its Phase 2 data (24.2% at 48 weeks) exceeded tirzepatide's Phase 3 results. The Phase 3 TRIUMPH-1 trial reported 28.3% mean weight loss at 80 weeks, with an extension subgroup reaching 30.3% at 104 weeks [10]. Notably, weight loss curves in Phase 2 had not plateaued at 48 weeks — suggesting longer treatment yields continued loss.

4. **Diabetes attenuates weight loss across all agents.** Patients with type 2 diabetes lose roughly 30–40% less weight than non-diabetic patients at equivalent doses. This is a consistent finding and reflects the metabolic complexity of established diabetes, not a failure of the drug.

### HbA1c Reduction (Glycemic Control)

| Trial | Agent | Dose | HbA1c Reduction | Population |
|-------|-------|------|-----------------|------------|
| SUSTAIN/SURPASS-2 [11] | Semaglutide | 1.0 mg | **−1.86%** | T2D |
| SURPASS-2 [11] | Tirzepatide | 15 mg | **−2.46%** | T2D (head-to-head) |
| TRANSCEND-T2D-1 [12] | Retatrutide | 12 mg | **−2.0%** | T2D (Phase 3) |

Tirzepatide produces the largest HbA1c reductions of the three, though all are highly effective. The ~0.6 percentage point advantage over semaglutide is clinically meaningful for diabetic patients targeting tight glycemic control.

### Responder Rates (≥15% Weight Loss)

| Agent | Dose | ≥15% Loss | ≥20% Loss | ≥25% Loss | Trial |
|-------|------|-----------|-----------|-----------|-------|
| Semaglutide | 2.4 mg | ~50% | ~32% | ~15% | STEP 1 |
| Tirzepatide | 15 mg | ~57% | ~50% | ~34% | SURMOUNT-1 |
| Retatrutide | 12 mg | ~83% | ~65% | ~48% | Phase 2 |

The responder data tell an important story: these are not guaranteed outcomes. Even with retatrutide, ~17% of participants did not achieve 15% weight loss. With semaglutide, half of participants failed to reach that threshold. Individual biology — genetics, baseline insulin resistance, gut microbiome, adherence, and lifestyle — drives enormous variation.

## Side Effect Profiles: The Cost of Efficacy

Greater receptor activation brings greater efficacy, but it also brings greater side effect burden. None of these agents are free lunches.

### Gastrointestinal Adverse Events

GI side effects dominate the tolerability profile of all three agents. They are dose-dependent, most severe during titration, and the primary reason for discontinuation.

| Side Effect | Semaglutide (STEP 1) | Tirzepatide (SURMOUNT-1) | Retatrutide (Phase 2) |
|-------------|----------------------|--------------------------|-----------------------|
| **Nausea** | 44% | 48% | 40–60% (dose-dependent) |
| **Diarrhea** | 31% | 23% | 20–30% |
| **Vomiting** | 24% | 24% | 15–25% |
| **Constipation** | 24% | 17% | 10–20% |
| **Discontinuation (any AE)** | 7% | 7–9% | 8–12% |

**Practical reality:** Most patients experience some GI discomfort in the first 4–8 weeks. For a minority, it is severe enough to stop treatment. Slow titration, hydration, small meals, and avoiding high-fat foods reduce but do not eliminate symptoms.

### Serious Risks

| Risk | Semaglutide | Tirzepatide | Retatrutide | Notes |
|------|-------------|-------------|-------------|-------|
| **Pancreatitis** | Rare, documented | Rare, documented | Under evaluation | Contraindicated in history of pancreatitis |
| **Gallbladder disease** | Increased risk | Increased risk | Under evaluation | Rapid weight loss itself contributes |
| **Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC)** | Boxed warning | Boxed warning | Likely boxed warning | Based on rodent data; human risk unproven |
| **MEN2** | Contraindicated | Contraindicated | Likely contraindicated | Personal/family history |
| **Hypoglycemia** | Low risk (monotherapy) | Low risk (monotherapy) | Low risk | Risk increases with insulin/sulfonylureas |
| **Heart rate increase** | +2–4 bpm | +2–4 bpm | +5–8 bpm | Retatrutide's glucagon effect may amplify this |
| **Injection site reactions** | 2–5% | 2–5% | 2–5% | Usually mild, self-limiting |

**Retatrutide-specific considerations:** The glucagon component produces a modest but consistent increase in resting heart rate (5–8 bpm). For patients with cardiovascular disease or arrhythmias, this requires monitoring. The long-term cardiovascular safety profile of triple agonism is being evaluated in dedicated outcomes trials.

### Other Notable Effects

- **Muscle loss:** All agents produce weight loss that includes lean mass. Resistance training and adequate protein intake (1.6–2.2 g/kg) are essential mitigations. The absolute muscle loss is typically 20–30% of total weight lost.
- **Facial aging ("Ozempic face"):** Rapid fat loss in facial subcutaneous tissue produces a gaunt appearance in some patients. This is a cosmetic consequence of efficacy, not a direct drug effect.
- **Gastroparesis:** Delayed gastric emptying can become pathological in susceptible individuals, particularly those with pre-existing motility disorders.
- **Suicidal ideation:** Rare cases have been reported. Patients with a history of depression require monitoring.

## Dosing Escalation Protocols

All three agents require gradual dose escalation. Starting at maximum dose produces intolerable nausea and vomiting in the majority of patients. The standard approach is a weekly subcutaneous injection with dose increases every 4 weeks.

### Semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy)

| Week | Dose | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| 1–4 | 0.25 mg | Initiation dose — minimal efficacy, tolerability assessment |
| 5–8 | 0.5 mg | Early glycemic effects; some appetite suppression |
| 9–12 | 1.0 mg | Meaningful weight loss begins for most patients |
| 13–16 | 1.7 mg | Intermediate step (Wegovy only) |
| 17+ | 2.4 mg | Target maintenance dose for weight management |

**Total titration time:** 16–20 weeks to reach 2.4 mg.

**Diabetes vs obesity dosing:** Ozempic (diabetes) is approved up to 2.0 mg. Wegovy (obesity) is approved up to 2.4 mg. In practice, many clinicians use Ozempic off-label for weight management at 1.0–2.0 mg due to cost and availability constraints.

### Tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound)

| Week | Dose | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| 1–4 | 2.5 mg | Initiation only — not an effective therapeutic dose |
| 5–8 | 5 mg | Minimum effective dose for some patients |
| 9–12 | 7.5 mg | Intermediate step |
| 13–16 | 10 mg | Strong efficacy for many patients |
| 17–20 | 12.5 mg | Pre-maximum step |
| 21+ | 15 mg | Maximum approved dose |

**Total titration time:** 20–24 weeks to reach 15 mg.

**Key point:** The 2.5 mg starting dose is purely for tolerability. Patients often become frustrated at 4–8 weeks when weight loss is minimal. Clinicians should set expectations: meaningful effects typically begin at 5–7.5 mg.

### Retatrutide (Investigational)

Retatrutide dosing is based on Phase 2 and Phase 3 trial protocols. It is not yet approved, so these are research reference doses only [3, 10].

| Week | Dose | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| 1–4 | 1–2 mg | Initiation |
| 5–8 | 4 mg | Early therapeutic range |
| 9–12 | 8 mg | Strong efficacy |
| 13+ | 12 mg | Maximum studied dose |

**Total titration time:** 12–16 weeks to reach 12 mg.

**Important:** Retatrutide is not available as a registered medicine. Any dosing information is derived from clinical trial publications and should not be used to guide self-administration.

### Dosing Comparison Summary

| Parameter | Semaglutide | Tirzepatide | Retatrutide |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|-------------|
| **Starting dose** | 0.25 mg | 2.5 mg | 1–2 mg |
| **Maximum dose** | 2.4 mg | 15 mg | 12 mg |
| **Titration steps** | 4–5 | 5–6 | 3–4 |
| **Time to max dose** | 16–20 weeks | 20–24 weeks | 12–16 weeks |
| **Injection frequency** | Weekly | Weekly | Weekly |
| **Route** | Subcutaneous | Subcutaneous | Subcutaneous |

### South African Pricing and Access

**Semaglutide (Ozempic):**
- **Private prescription cost:** R8,000–R12,000 per month for 1.0–2.0 mg doses, depending on pharmacy and dose
- **Medical aid:** Some schemes cover Ozempic for type 2 diabetes under chronic medicine benefits. Coverage for obesity (Wegovy) is extremely limited in South Africa
- **Availability:** Registered by SAHPRA. Available through most private pharmacies, though stock shortages have occurred due to global demand

**Tirzepatide:**
- **Private prescription cost:** Not formally available through registered channels. Grey-market compounded or imported versions range from R6,000–R15,000 per month depending on dose and source
- **Medical aid:** No coverage — not registered by SAHPRA
- **Availability:** Not SAHPRA-registered as of June 2026. Access is through personal importation, compounding pharmacies operating in grey areas, or international suppliers

**Retatrutide:**
- **Cost:** No registered product exists. Research-grade or grey-market pricing is speculative but likely R8,000–R20,000+ per month if sourced
- **Medical aid:** No coverage
- **Availability:** Not SAHPRA-registered. Not FDA-approved. Access is limited to clinical trial participation or unregulated grey-market sourcing

### The Compounding Reality

In South Africa, some compounding pharmacies prepare semaglutide and tirzepatide formulations at lower cost (R3,000–R8,000/month). Quality is highly variable. Compounded products are not subject to SAHPRA batch testing, stability validation, or sterility assurance at the same standard as registered medicines. We have covered quality verification in our [SAHPRA regulations article](/blog/sahpra-peptide-regulations).

### The Ozempic Shortage Problem

South Africa has not been immune to the global semaglutide shortage. From 2023 through 2025, demand from both diabetic patients and off-label weight-loss users created intermittent stock-outs at major pharmacy chains. Novo Nordisk has prioritized high-income markets, and SA supply has been inconsistent.

**Practical implications:**
- Diabetic patients on Ozempic have faced interrupted therapy
- Off-label weight-loss users compete with diabetics for limited stock
- Prices have risen above listed levels during shortage periods
- Some patients have turned to compounded or grey-market alternatives of uncertain quality

### Importing for Personal Use

South African law allows personal importation of unregistered medicines under certain conditions, but this is not a straightforward process for Schedule medicines. Because GLP-1 agonists are not scheduled by SAHPRA (they are registered prescription medicines, not controlled substances), the legal framework is ambiguous.

**What we know:**
- SARS customs generally tolerates small personal-quantity imports of non-controlled medicines
- A valid foreign prescription strengthens your position if questioned
- Commercial quantities (more than 3 months' supply) attract scrutiny
- Compounded peptides shipped without temperature control may degrade in transit

**Risk:** Importing unregistered medicines for personal use exists in a grey area. It is not explicitly prohibited for unscheduled substances, but it is not formally protected either. See our [SAHPRA regulations guide](/blog/sahpra-peptide-regulations) for a deeper analysis.

## Who Should Use Which: A Decision Framework

There is no universally "best" agent. The right choice depends on patient characteristics, goals, comorbidities, and practical constraints.

### Choose Semaglutide If:

- You have **type 2 diabetes** and need proven glycemic control with cardiovascular outcome data (SELECT trial showed 20% MACE reduction [13])
- You want the **most established safety profile** — two decades of GLP-1 data, largest real-world exposure
- You have **limited budget** and need a registered, pharmacy-available product
- You have **cardiovascular disease** — semaglutide has the strongest CV outcomes evidence
- You are **sensitive to side effects** — single-receptor agonism may produce less nausea than dual/triple agents at equivalent efficacy
- You need **an oral option** — oral semaglutide (Rybelsus) exists for diabetes, though weight loss is inferior to injectable

### Choose Tirzepatide If:

- Your **primary goal is weight loss** and you want the best balance of efficacy and availability
- You have **type 2 diabetes** and want superior HbA1c reduction
- You have **insulin resistance or PCOS** — the GIP component may offer metabolic advantages
- You have **obstructive sleep apnea** — tirzepatide has specific FDA approval for OSA in obesity
- Semaglutide **stopped working or was insufficient** — stepping up receptor activation often breaks plateaus
- You can **tolerate a longer titration** and slightly higher GI side effect risk

### Consider Retatrutide If:

- You are enrolled in a **clinical trial** — this is the only legitimate access pathway currently
- You have **severe obesity (BMI >35)** and need the highest efficacy ceiling
- You have **MASH/NAFLD** — early data shows substantial liver fat reduction
- You have **failed both semaglutide and tirzepatide** — triple agonism represents a mechanistic escalation
- You understand you are using an **investigational agent** with incomplete long-term safety data

### Do Not Use Any of These If:

- You have a **personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC)** or MEN2
- You have a **history of pancreatitis**
- You are **pregnant, planning pregnancy, or breastfeeding**
- You have **severe gastroparesis** or inflammatory bowel disease with obstruction risk
- You have **type 1 diabetes** — these agents are not insulin replacements
- You are seeking a **"quick fix" without lifestyle change** — these drugs augment, not replace, behavioral intervention

### Absolute Contraindications

| Condition | Semaglutide | Tirzepatide | Retatrutide |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|-------------|
| Personal/family history of MTC | Contraindicated | Contraindicated | Contraindicated (expected) |
| MEN2 | Contraindicated | Contraindicated | Contraindicated (expected) |
| Prior pancreatitis | Contraindicated | Contraindicated | Contraindicated (expected) |
| Pregnancy | Contraindicated | Contraindicated | Contraindicated (expected) |
| Severe gastroparesis | Contraindicated | Contraindicated | Contraindicated (expected) |

### Important Drug Interactions

- **Insulin and sulfonylureas:** All three increase hypoglycemia risk when combined. Dose reduction of insulin/sulfonylurea is typically required.
- **Oral medications:** Delayed gastric emptying can reduce absorption of oral drugs. Take oral contraceptives, thyroid medication, and other critical oral drugs at least 1 hour before or 4 hours after GLP-1 injection.
- **Warfarin:** Rapid weight loss and dietary changes alter vitamin K intake. INR monitoring is essential.
- **Alcohol:** Exacerbates nausea and gastritis. Best minimized during titration.

## The Honest Bottom Line

These three agents represent genuine advances in metabolic medicine. They are not placebos, not scams, and not minor improvements. Semaglutide changed the standard of care. Tirzepatide raised the bar further. Retatrutide may redefine what pharmacotherapy can achieve.

But the hype has outpaced the evidence in several important ways:

1. **Long-term durability beyond 2 years is uncertain for all three.** STEP 5 showed sustained loss at 104 weeks, but what happens at 5 years? We do not know.

2. **Weight regain after discontinuation is substantial.** SURMOUNT-4 showed that stopping tirzepatide led to regaining ~70% of lost weight within a year. These are chronic therapies, not cures.

3. **Real-world effectiveness is lower than trial efficacy.** Trial participants receive intensive lifestyle support, frequent monitoring, and free medication. Real-world adherence and outcomes are inferior.

4. **The South African access gap is real.** Registered semaglutide is expensive and intermittently unavailable. Tirzepatide and retatrutide are not registered. Grey-market sourcing carries quality, legal, and safety risks.

5. **Individual response varies enormously.** Genetics, baseline metabolic health, adherence, sleep, stress, and concurrent medications all modulate outcomes. Trial averages are not personal guarantees.

For South African researchers, the priority should be: understanding these agents deeply, sourcing responsibly if studying them, and advocating for regulatory frameworks that balance access with safety. For patients, the priority should be: medical supervision, realistic expectations, and a commitment to the lifestyle behaviors that these drugs make possible but do not replace.
